Lab 5: Interactive Network Visualization

1. Dataset Description

This synthetic urban transit network contains 50 stations and 50 direct routes. Stations have a district, average daily passenger volume, and station type. Routes have a travel time and service type. Connections are undirected: a route from A to B also connects B to A. The layout shows connectivity, not a geographic map.

2. Node-Link Visualization

Drag a station to untangle routes. Hover over a station to reveal its direct neighbors, or hover over a route for its endpoints and travel time. Numbers identify stations (for example, 7 means Station 7). Use the selector for a persistent highlight.

3. Node-Link Design Description

I used different fill colors for districts because these are categories with no natural ranking. Circle area represents daily passengers, so busier stations look larger. I kept all stations circular to make size comparisons easier and used thin, thick, or dashed borders for Local, Transfer, and Terminal stations. Route color runs from light to dark blue as travel time increases. All routes have the same width: a longer journey does not mean a stronger connection. Solid, dashed, and dotted lines distinguish Metro, Express, and Shuttle services. Dragging helps separate nearby stations. Hovering fades unrelated stations and routes, making direct neighbors easier to follow. Tooltips give full station names, passenger counts, station types, degrees, and exact route times. Node position reflects the force layout rather than geography or a calculated importance score.

4. Adjacency Matrix

Rows and columns are stations, grouped by district and then station number. A colored cell is a direct route; a pale gray cell is no direct route. The diagonal is darker gray (same station). Hover over any cell to identify its row and column. Scroll horizontally on smaller screens.

Hover over a matrix cell to inspect a station pair.

5. Matrix Design Description

The matrix uses the same stations on both axes. A colored cell means a direct route, and each undirected route appears twice, mirrored across the diagonal. Cell hue identifies service type, while darker cells indicate longer travel times. District-colored labels and district boundaries make connections between groups easier to compare. Within each district, station numbers increase so names are easy to find. I left passenger volume and station type out of the cells to avoid overloading them. Hover outlines the relevant row and column and reports the exact pair and route information. This view helps inspect direct connections and district patterns, although following a journey through several stations is easier in the node-link view.

6. Network Questions and Findings

  1. Which stations appear central in the network?
    Partially · Both views. Hover highlighting reveals branching stations such as 7, 16, 23, 29, and 31. Each has three direct neighbors, also visible as three colored cells in its matrix row. Several stations share this degree, so there is no single dominant hub. A station near the middle of the drawing is not necessarily the most central: the force layout changes when dragged. These views support local connectivity, but do not directly rank betweenness or other centrality measures.
  2. Which districts are strongly connected to one another?
    Well for direct-route counts · Adjacency matrix. The district boundaries make the South–East block look more populated than the other district-pair blocks. Counting its colored cells gives 11 direct routes, compared with 9 each for Central–North and East–West. Since every district has 10 stations, these blocks are comparable in size. “Strongly connected” here means more direct routes, not greater passenger flow or a formal directed-network property.
  3. Where are transfer or terminal stations located in the topology?
    Well · Node-link. Thick-bordered Transfer stations occur along the network and at some endpoints: Stations 44, 47, and 50 each have one attached route. Dashed-border Terminal stations do not consistently mark ends: Station 7 branches to three neighbors, while 43, 46, and 49 stand apart without links. Border styles and neighborhood hover reveal that the synthetic role labels do not reliably predict topological position.
  4. Which stations have high passenger volume?
    Well · Node-link. Stations 46–50 are among the largest circles, with Station 50 the largest. Its tooltip confirms 9,850 daily passengers. The large isolated circles for 46, 48, and 49 show why passenger volume should not be confused with degree. Size reveals the high-volume group; tooltips distinguish the small differences between similarly sized stations.
  5. Where are the longest direct travel-time connections?
    Well with hover · Both views. The darkest blue routes include Station 7–31, Station 18–34, and Station 29–50. Hover confirms 16, 15, and 14 minutes respectively. In the matrix, these are dark orange Express cells in the North–Central, South–East, and East–West blocks. Darkness helps locate candidates and tooltips establish the exact ranking; physical line length does not encode travel time.
  6. Are particular route types concentrated in particular parts of the network?
    Partially · Adjacency matrix. The major district-pair blocks mix teal Metro, orange Express, and purple Shuttle cells rather than separating into single-service regions. A small exception is the Central–Central block: its two routes, Station 1–16 and Station 21–41, are both purple Shuttle cells. Hover confirms their service type. This supports a local pattern, but two connections are too few to claim a broad service specialization.
